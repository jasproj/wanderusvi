Annaberg Sugar Plantation Ruins, St. John

Updated October 2026 · WanderUSVI Tours you can book here →

In the 1700s, St. John was almost entirely cleared of its original forest to grow sugarcane. At its peak, over 100 plantations operated on the island — a small, steep island with no natural flat ground and barely enough rainfall for reliable harvests. Almost all of that history has been reclaimed by the jungle. The Annaberg Plantation is the exception: the best-preserved sugar estate in the USVI, maintained by the National Park Service as a free-access historical site on the north shore of St. John.

What You'll See

The complex covers the main functions of a working sugar estate. The windmill tower — circular stone, about 30 feet tall, built to power the grinding rollers that crushed cane — still stands on the ridge above the water. Next to it is the horse mill, a lower circular structure used when wind was insufficient. Below the mills, the factory building contains the remnants of the boiling house, where cane juice was rendered down through a series of copper kettles into crystallized sugar and molasses for rum.

Downhill from the factory, the outlines of the enslaved workers' village are visible — stone foundations of small houses, arranged on the terraces below the factory. Interpretive signs throughout the site explain what each structure was used for and how it fit into the production chain. Annaberg was built around 1718 and operated for over a century, producing sugar and rum using the labor of enslaved Africans who were brought to the island by the Danish colonial authorities.

History in Context

The Danish West Indies were one of the most intensively farmed sugar territories in the Caribbean. St. John, in particular, was the site of a major slave revolt in 1733 — one of the longest-lasting in the history of the Americas — when enslaved Akan people seized control of the island for several months before Danish and French forces combined to suppress it. That history is visible at Annaberg in the watchtower — a structure positioned to monitor the landscape and signal plantation-to-plantation communication.

The emancipation of enslaved people in the Danish West Indies came in 1848, following Governor Peter von Scholten's proclamation in response to a mass uprising in Frederiksted, St. Croix. After emancipation, most plantations on St. John were abandoned within a generation as former enslaved workers left and the sugar economy collapsed.

Visiting

Annaberg is free to visit with no reservation required. The site is open during daylight hours. There is a small parking area off North Shore Road. The walk through the ruins takes about 30 to 45 minutes. The Leinster Bay Trail begins at the parking area and continues down to a calm bay with excellent snorkeling and views toward the BVI — a natural extension of the visit.

Local tip: The NPS periodically offers living history demonstrations at Annaberg, with rangers or volunteers demonstrating traditional food preparation, basket weaving, and other colonial-era skills. Check the VINP visitor center schedule in Cruz Bay for current programs.

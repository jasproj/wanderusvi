Best Parks in the US Virgin Islands

Updated October 2026 · WanderUSVI

The US Virgin Islands hold a disproportionate concentration of federally protected land for their size. Six National Park Service units and one US Fish and Wildlife Service refuge protect beaches, coral reefs, submarine canyons, historical forts, and turtle nesting beaches across St. Thomas, St. John, and St. Croix. Most of St. John — roughly 60% of the island — falls inside Virgin Islands National Park. Buck Island Reef National Monument off St. Croix contains one of the healthiest elkhorn coral formations remaining in the Caribbean. Sandy Point National Wildlife Refuge protects a critical leatherback sea turtle nesting beach. This guide covers every protected area in the territory, what makes each worth visiting, and how to get there.

Virgin Islands National Park (St. John)

Virgin Islands National Park covers approximately 7,259 acres of land on St. John — roughly 60% of the island — plus around 5,650 acres of submerged reef and sea floor. Established in 1956 following a land donation by Laurance Rockefeller, the park also encompasses a portion of Hassel Island in St. Thomas Harbor. When you step off the ferry at Cruz Bay, you are essentially arriving at the park's front door.

The park's north shore is a sequence of bays that would individually anchor a destination resort elsewhere — Hawksnest, Trunk Bay, Cinnamon Bay, Maho Bay, Francis Bay. Trunk Bay is the most famous and charges a per-person entry fee; the rest are free. More than 20 maintained hiking trails cross the park, ranging from a 0.25-mile loop at Annaberg Plantation to the 2.2-mile Reef Bay Trail leading to pre-Columbian petroglyphs. See our complete guide to hiking trails in the USVI for full trail descriptions and difficulty ratings.

The park visitor center in Cruz Bay — located right off the ferry landing — has free maps, ranger schedules, and current conditions. It is worth stopping there before heading to any beach or trailhead. Ranger-led programs, including guided hikes to the Reef Bay petroglyphs with a boat return from the beach, are offered periodically and are among the best-value experiences in the park.

Buck Island Reef National Monument (St. Croix)

Buck Island Reef National Monument sits about 1.5 miles off the northeast coast of St. Croix. The monument encompasses the island, its surrounding reef, and an extensive area of open water protecting one of the most significant elkhorn coral formations remaining in the US Caribbean. An underwater snorkel trail is the centerpiece — a marked route through coral in relatively shallow, clear water that is accessible to most swimmers.

Access is exclusively by boat. Licensed tour operators depart from Christiansted Harbor on St. Croix with half-day and full-day options. The NPS does not operate its own ferry to the monument. Most tours include equipment, instruction, and a stop at the island's beach. The monument is free once you arrive; you pay the tour operator for transport. If you are visiting St. Croix, this is one of the most worthwhile half-days you can spend.

Salt River Bay National Historical Park & Ecological Preserve (St. Croix)

Salt River Bay on St. Croix's north shore is simultaneously one of the most historically significant and least-visited NPS units in the territory. In 1493, Christopher Columbus and his crew made the first documented European contact with indigenous people on what is now US soil here — a violent encounter with the Taino and Carib peoples that preceded Spanish colonization. The bay is designated both a national historical park and an ecological preserve, protecting the watershed, mangroves, coral reefs, and the entrance to a submarine canyon.

The submarine canyon at Salt River Bay is a significant geological feature — a deep-water trench that begins close to the shoreline and plunges thousands of feet. Divers know Salt River as one of the most dramatic wall dive destinations in the territory. The mangrove channels that border the bay are excellent for kayaking, and paddling tours regularly run through the protected estuary. There are no entrance fees and limited visitor infrastructure — this is a raw, largely undeveloped park unit, which is part of its appeal.

Virgin Islands Coral Reef National Monument (St. John)

Established in 2001, Virgin Islands Coral Reef National Monument protects thousands of acres of submerged lands along the south shore and offshore areas of St. John. The monument was created specifically to protect coral reef ecosystems that were not already covered by the national park's original boundaries. It includes healthy reef systems, sea grass beds, and the open-water habitat used by sea turtles, reef fish, and migratory species. Unlike the national park, the monument has no visitor center or managed access point — it is simply a federal designation protecting the reef from anchoring, fishing, and development.

Sandy Point National Wildlife Refuge (St. Croix)

Sandy Point occupies the western tip of St. Croix and is one of the most important leatherback sea turtle nesting beaches in the western hemisphere. Leatherbacks, the largest sea turtle species, come ashore here typically from late spring through early summer to nest, and hatchlings emerge through mid-summer. During the active nesting and hatching period the beach is closed to visitors to protect the turtles; outside that window it is open as a public beach — a sweeping stretch of white sand with no facilities and complete solitude.

Contact the US Fish and Wildlife Service or check their website for exact closure dates before planning a visit — the timing varies year to year with the turtles. Sandy Point can be combined with the Frederick E. Dorsch Beachfront Park in nearby Frederiksted for a full day on St. Croix's west coast.

Christiansted National Historic Site (St. Croix)

Christiansted National Historic Site preserves the colonial waterfront of Christiansted, the former capital of the Danish West Indies. The site includes Fort Christiansvaern — a bright yellow Danish fort built in the 1740s and one of the best-preserved colonial forts in the Caribbean — along with several adjacent historic structures around the harbor. The fort charges a small entry fee (verify current amount at the park); the surrounding historic district is free to walk.

The NPS visitor center at the wharf has exhibits on the Danish colonial period, the enslaved labor that built the sugar economy, and the US purchase of the islands in 1917. This is a compact, walkable historic site that pairs well with an afternoon exploring Christiansted's restaurants and galleries.

Territory & Local Parks

Magens Bay (St. Thomas)

Magens Bay is the most famous beach in the USVI and is managed by the Magens Bay Authority, a charitable trust rather than a federal or government body. The $5 per-person entry fee covers parking, restrooms, showers, and facilities maintenance. The bay is protected by hills on three sides, making it one of the calmest swimming spots on St. Thomas regardless of wind conditions. A nature trail runs along the eastern edge of the beach through coastal woodland. Arrive before 10 AM or after 3 PM on cruise ship days to avoid peak crowds.

St. George Village Botanical Garden (St. Croix)

Built over the ruins of an 18th-century Danish sugar estate, St. George Village Botanical Garden is a 16-acre private nonprofit garden with more than 1,500 plant species arranged across the original stone foundations of the plantation. The ruins — boiling houses, slave quarters, and a great house — are integrated into the garden paths. An on-site museum covers the site's colonial history. Check their website for current admission fees and hours, which can vary seasonally.

When to Go

Most USVI parks are open and accessible year-round. Peak season (December through April) brings the clearest water, best hiking weather (low humidity, trade wind cooling), and lightest rain. The dry-season months of February through April are ideal for both land and water activities. Summer brings hotter, more humid conditions on trails and increases in afternoon rain; water visibility is slightly lower but still good. Hurricane season runs June through November — the parks remain open but operators may cancel water-based activities during storm events.

Sandy Point NWR is the main seasonal exception: plan your visit outside the leatherback turtle nesting closure (typically spring through midsummer, with the exact window varying by year). Buck Island is year-round but half-day tours are weather-dependent and may not run in rough seas.

What to Bring

  • Water: At least 1 liter per person per hour on any trail. Most parks have no potable water except at main facilities.
  • Sun protection: Year-round UV is intense. Rash guards are more practical than sunscreen on water-based activities.
  • Reef-safe sunscreen: Required for water activities near coral; non-reef-safe formulas are illegal for sale in the USVI and harmful to the reefs.
  • Snorkel gear: Bring your own or rent in Cruz Bay or Christiansted. Gear quality from rental shops varies.
  • America the Beautiful pass: Covers Trunk Bay and all NPS fee sites in the territory.
Local tip: The VINP Visitor Center in Cruz Bay is the best single resource for current trail conditions, ranger programs, and water closures. It is free, open daily, and staffed by rangers who know exactly what is blooming, nesting, or temporarily closed. Stop there before heading anywhere on St. John.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many national parks are in the US Virgin Islands?

The USVI has multiple NPS units: Virgin Islands National Park (St. John), Buck Island Reef National Monument (St. Croix), Salt River Bay NHP & Ecological Preserve (St. Croix), Virgin Islands Coral Reef National Monument (offshore St. John), and Christiansted National Historic Site (St. Croix). Sandy Point National Wildlife Refuge (St. Croix) is managed by the US Fish and Wildlife Service. Together, they protect a remarkable share of the islands' land and marine environment.

Is Virgin Islands National Park free?

Most of the park is free — all hiking trails, most beaches, and the visitor center. Trunk Bay charges a per-person entry fee. The America the Beautiful Annual Pass covers Trunk Bay and all other NPS fee sites. Check the NPS website for current fee amounts before your visit.

Can you visit Buck Island without a boat?

No. Buck Island sits about 1.5 miles offshore and is only reachable by boat. Licensed tour operators run half-day and full-day trips from Christiansted Harbor. The monument itself is free; you pay for transport. Most tours include snorkel gear and instruction.

What is Sandy Point National Wildlife Refuge?

Sandy Point NWR on St. Croix's western tip protects one of the Caribbean's most important leatherback sea turtle nesting beaches. The beach closes during the nesting and hatching season (typically spring through early summer; check the USFWS website for exact dates). Outside the closure it is open for swimming and walking — a wild, facilities-free stretch of beach with no crowds.

Do you need a passport to visit USVI national parks?

No. The USVI is a US territory. US citizens do not need a passport and can use standard domestic ID. The America the Beautiful pass works at all NPS fee sites. Note: the British Virgin Islands, visible from St. Thomas, do require a passport for US citizens — that is a separate country.

What is the best park for snorkeling in the USVI?

Buck Island Reef National Monument has the most celebrated snorkeling — a marked underwater trail through large elkhorn coral in very clear water. Virgin Islands National Park's north shore beaches (Trunk Bay, Hawksnest Bay, Waterlemon Cay) are excellent for shore snorkeling with healthy coral close to the beach.

What is Salt River Bay?

Salt River Bay NHP on St. Croix preserves the site of the first documented European contact with indigenous people on what is now US soil (Columbus, 1493). It is also a significant ecological site with mangroves, reefs, and a submarine canyon that makes it one of the territory's top dive destinations. Kayaking the mangrove channels is the most popular visitor activity.

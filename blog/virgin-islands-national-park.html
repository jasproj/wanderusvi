Virgin Islands National Park, St. John

Updated October 2026 · WanderUSVI Tours you can book here →

Most US national parks have a fence around them. Virgin Islands National Park doesn't — it is most of the island. About 60% of St. John's land area, roughly 7,259 acres, falls within park boundaries, along with around 5,650 acres of submerged reef and sea floor. When you take the ferry from St. Thomas and step off in Cruz Bay, you are essentially arriving at the park's front door.

The park was established in 1956, following a land donation by Laurance Rockefeller, who had been quietly purchasing land on the island since the early 1950s. The result is an island where most of the shoreline, most of the forest, and all of the best hiking trails are protected federal land — and where you can drive across the entire width in 20 minutes but never feel like you've left wilderness.

Beaches

The north shore road runs through a sequence of bays that could individually anchor a resort — Hawksnest, Trunk Bay, Cinnamon Bay, Maho Bay, Francis Bay — and most are within the park. Trunk Bay is the most famous and charges an entrance fee. Hawksnest is free and often less crowded, with reef close to shore. Cinnamon Bay has the longest stretch of sand. Francis Bay is peaceful and known for good birdwatching at the salt pond behind the beach. Maho Bay is the best spot on St. John to see sea turtles feeding on sea grass — they appear reliably near the shore.

Hiking

The park has over 20 maintained hiking trails. The Reef Bay Trail is the signature hike — 2.3 miles downhill from Centerline Road through three distinct vegetation zones to a beach, with a side trail to pre-Columbian petroglyphs carved beside a freshwater pool. The Leinster Bay Trail leads to the Annaberg sugar mill ruins. The Ram Head Trail at the south end of the island crosses dry scrub forest to a dramatic basalt promontory above the sea. The Bordeaux Mountain Trail climbs to the island's high point at around 1,277 feet for views across the Virgins passage.

Reef and Marine Life

The submerged lands within the park contain some of the healthiest coral in the territory. The absence of cruise ships on St. John (none dock here), combined with park protection since the 1950s, has given the reefs more recovery time than comparable sites on St. Thomas. Snorkeling from any north shore beach puts you on live coral within a few fin kicks of the sand.

Historical Sites

St. John was heavily settled and farmed during the Danish colonial period. The park contains the remains of roughly 100 sugar and cotton estates — overgrown stone walls, windmill towers, and processing buildings that appear suddenly along trails and roadsides. Annaberg Plantation, on the north shore, is the most preserved and the only site with interpretive signage. The Reef Bay Trail passes plantation ruins along the way down.

Local tip: The VINP Visitor Center in Cruz Bay (right off the ferry dock) has free maps, trail conditions, and ranger-led program schedules. Stop there first — ranger-led hikes to the Reef Bay petroglyphs are offered periodically and include a boat return from the beach at the bottom of the trail.

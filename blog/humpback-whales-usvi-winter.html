Humpback Whales in the USVI

Updated October 2026 · WanderUSVI Tours you can book here →

Every winter, humpback whales pass through the waters of the US Virgin Islands. They are traveling north — finishing their breeding season near the Silver Bank off the Dominican Republic and heading toward summer feeding grounds in the North Atlantic. The passage takes them through the deep-water channels around St. Thomas and St. John, and for a few weeks between January and March, there is a real chance of encountering them from a boat.

This is not New England whale watching. The USVI is not a feeding ground, and the whales are in transit. But the animals are large, the encounters can be dramatic, and the backdrop — the same turquoise water and green hills you're looking at all week — makes a humpback breach feel genuinely surreal.

The Migration Route

Humpback whales in the western North Atlantic spend their winters in the warm shallow waters of the Caribbean, primarily around the Silver Bank (a submerged plateau north of the Dominican Republic) and Samana Bay. These are breeding and calving grounds — whales come here to mate and give birth, not to feed. By late January and February, they begin moving north toward summer feeding areas in the Gulf of Maine, around Iceland, and off Norway.

The passage north takes them through the Mona Passage (between Puerto Rico and Hispaniola) and the waters north of the USVI. The Anegada Passage — the deep-water channel between the Virgin Islands and the open Atlantic — is a natural corridor on this route. Whales sighted in USVI waters are generally transiting individuals or small groups, occasionally mothers with calves heading north for the first time.

What to Expect on a Whale Watching Trip

Whale watching from St. Thomas is done on private or charter boats that head offshore into deeper water, north and northwest of the island. The animals are most likely to be seen from the surface — blows first, then dorsals, occasionally full breaches. Humpbacks are acoustically active during breeding season and males near the USVI may still be singing, though you would need hydrophone equipment on the boat to hear it.

Encounters are real but not guaranteed. Responsible operators follow federal marine mammal protection guidelines, which require maintaining specific minimum distances from the animals and prohibit pursuit or interception. The whales control the interaction. When encounters happen, they tend to be brief but memorable — a few minutes of surfacing, breathing, and diving before the animals move on.

Season and Planning

Peak season is February through early March, aligning with the height of the northern migration. January can produce sightings but is less reliable. By April, most animals have moved on. If whale watching is a priority, plan your USVI trip for February. The same season coincides with the USVI's dry-season weather window — low humidity, consistent trade winds, and minimal rain — so the conditions are good for offshore boating regardless.

Local tip: Sea conditions north of St. Thomas can be rougher than the sheltered bays you might snorkel in. Whale watching trips head into open water where Atlantic swells can be significant. If you're prone to seasickness, take appropriate precautions before boarding.

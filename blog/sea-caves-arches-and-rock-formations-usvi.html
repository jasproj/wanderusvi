Hidden Places, Sea Caves & Rock Formations of the Virgin Islands

Updated October 2026 · WanderUSVI Tours you can book here →

The most dramatic sea caves in the Virgin Islands are in the British Virgin Islands — The Caves at Norman Island and the granite grottos of The Baths at Virgin Gorda are world-famous dive and snorkel sites, accessible on a day trip from St. Thomas. The USVI's own geological highlights tend to be underwater: submarine canyon walls, volcanic sea stacks, and reef formations colonized with life. On land, the "hidden places" worth seeking out are historical — pre-Columbian rock carvings and 300-year-old sugar plantation ruins that appear without warning along forest trails. This guide covers both, organized honestly: what is in the USVI and accessible without a passport, and what requires a day trip to the BVI.

BVI note: The British Virgin Islands are a separate country. Visiting any BVI site — including The Caves at Norman Island and The Baths at Virgin Gorda — requires a valid US passport and an international border crossing. Day trip boats from St. Thomas handle the customs process. See our BVI day trip guide for details.

USVI Underwater Rock Formations

Carvel Rock

Carvel Rock rises from the water several miles south of St. Thomas and is visible from the island's southern shoreline on clear days. The formation is a tall, isolated sea stack that creates upwelling currents and attracts a concentration of marine life — large fish, coral, sea birds nesting on the surface, and the occasional passing shark or ray. It is primarily a dive site, with the rock's underwater structure providing ledges, overhangs, and current-swept walls. Access is by boat only; most charter operators in St. Thomas can take divers or snorkelers there on a clear-weather day. See our complete USVI diving guide for site details and operator recommendations.

Congo Cay

Congo Cay is a small rocky island off the eastern tip of St. Thomas, sitting in the channel between St. Thomas and St. John. It is a well-known snorkel and dive site — the current-swept passage around the cay creates a diverse reef with sea fans, large sponges, and the strong fish traffic that accompanies current-washed formations. Reef sharks and barracuda are regular visitors. The island itself is nesting habitat for brown boobies and other sea birds. Boats pass it frequently on the way to St. John or on charter trips out of Red Hook. A calm weather day makes the snorkel much more enjoyable; the current can be significant.

Waterlemon Cay (St. John)

Waterlemon Cay is a small rocky island sitting just offshore in Leinster Bay on St. John's north shore, about 100 yards from the Annaberg Plantation parking area. The snorkeling around the cay is among the best shore-accessible snorkeling in Virgin Islands National Park — sea turtles, spotted eagle rays, and dense reef fish populations in relatively calm, clear water. Wade in from the small beach at the Annaberg trailhead, swim across the flat, and circle the cay. The south side typically has the most activity. This is an easy, free, and undervisited snorkel that most visitors miss because the Annaberg ruins are the listed attraction in the parking lot, not the cay offshore.

Salt River Canyon (St. Croix)

The submarine canyon at Salt River Bay on St. Croix's north shore is a geological feature on a different scale than the reef dives at other USVI sites. The bay mouth drops into a deep-water canyon with two distinct wall dive profiles: the East Wall, where the drop-off is dramatic and open to the deep, and the West Wall, a more enclosed set of passages, overhangs, and tunnels encrusted in orange cup coral and soft corals. Large pelagics — including hammerhead sharks — patrol the deeper water. This is also the site of Columbus's documented 1493 landing on what is now US soil, preserved within Salt River Bay National Historical Park. Kayaking the mangrove estuary within the bay is a separate, non-diving way to experience the site. See our USVI diving guide for diver details and operator recommendations.

Frederiksted Pier (St. Croix)

The Frederiksted Pier is a functioning cruise ship pier on St. Croix's west coast that has, over decades, become one of the most celebrated pier dive sites in the Caribbean. The pilings are thickly encrusted with sponges, soft corals, and hydroids; the understructure creates a complex artificial reef habitat sheltering seahorses, frogfish, batfish, spotted drum, and dozens of other species that rely on structure and low light. Night dives here are internationally known — bioluminescent organisms, octopus, lobsters, and moray eels emerge after dark. Day dives are better for photography and macro subjects. The pier is accessible from shore (a ladder provides entry) or by dive boat. The maximum depth is around 40 feet, making this accessible to Open Water certified divers.

Hidden Historical Sites on Land

Reef Bay Petroglyphs (St. John)

The Reef Bay petroglyphs are the most significant pre-Columbian site accessible to visitors in the USVI. Located beside a freshwater pool at the base of the Reef Bay Trail — a 2.2-mile hike down from Centerline Road — the carvings include geometric forms and anthropomorphic faces attributed to the Taino people. They are among the only surviving examples of pre-Columbian rock art in the US Virgin Islands. The carvings are at water level and partially submerged when the freshwater pool is full; the best viewing is from above or from the edge of the pool. The NPS offers periodic ranger-led hikes to the petroglyphs that include interpretation and a boat return from the beach at the trail's end — avoiding the steep uphill return. See our USVI hiking guide for trail notes.

Annaberg Plantation Ruins (St. John)

The Annaberg sugar estate on St. John's north shore is the best-preserved plantation ruin in the territory, with an intact windmill tower, horse mill, boiling house, and slave quarters still standing above the bay. Interpretive signs explain the Danish sugar economy and the enslaved labor that built it. The site is free to enter, open daily, and requires only a short walk from the parking area. It is also next to the Waterlemon Cay snorkel access, making the two natural companions for a half-day. The view from the windmill tower over Leinster Bay and toward the BVI is one of the better panoramic views on St. John.

Point Udall (St. Croix)

The eastern tip of St. Croix is the easternmost point in the United States, marked by a millennium monument and offering an unobstructed view east into the open Atlantic. The geological character of the point — windswept limestone cliffs, crashing surf, and a treeless headland — is unlike anything else in the USVI, which tends toward green hills and calm coves. It is accessible by car to a small parking area, with a short walk to the point itself. A hidden beach below the cliffs (Isaac Bay) can be reached by a longer scramble and is one of the most remote and least-visited beaches in the territory.

BVI Sea Caves and Formations — Via Day Trip

The most celebrated cave and rock formation experiences in the Virgin Islands region require crossing to the British Virgin Islands. This is a passport-required international crossing, but day trip boats from St. Thomas and St. John handle the customs process and make the journey straightforward. See our complete BVI day trip guide for logistics.

The Caves — Norman Island

Norman Island sits in the Sir Francis Drake Channel in the BVI, southwest of Tortola. Three sea caves cut into the cliff face on the island's west side — The Caves — are accessible by boat and snorkel. The cave openings face the water and are large enough to swim into; inside, the walls are encrusted with orange sponge, tube worms, and orange cup coral. Schools of glassy sweepers fill the caves in the afternoon light. Snorkeling the cave mouths is the primary activity; scuba divers can go deeper into the cave structures. The pirate treasure legends associated with Norman Island (said to have inspired Stevenson's Treasure Island) add to the mystique. Accessible on day trip boats from St. Thomas.

The Baths — Virgin Gorda

The Baths at the southern tip of Virgin Gorda are the most famous geological formation in the entire Virgin Islands region. Ancient granite boulders — some the size of houses — have tumbled together at the shoreline, and seawater fills the passages between them to create a series of pools, tunnels, and grottos you explore by wading and scrambling. The trail from the north beach through The Baths to Devil's Bay on the south side is short but genuinely adventurous — ducking under boulders, wading through pools, climbing over formations. Virgin Gorda is in the BVI; passport required. The site is the most visited in the BVI and can be crowded mid-morning when multiple day trip boats arrive simultaneously.

The Indians — BVI

The Indians are four rock pinnacles rising from the sea off Norman Island in the BVI. Underwater, the pinnacles extend to about 40 feet and are covered in corals, sea fans, and sponges; the current-swept sides attract pelagic species — barracuda, tarpon, and reef sharks. Most BVI day trips include The Indians as a snorkel stop. The pinnacles are visible above the surface and make for a dramatic approach by boat.

Access and Logistics

USVI underwater formations are accessed through dive and snorkel charter operators based in Charlotte Amalie (St. Thomas), Red Hook (St. Thomas), Cruz Bay (St. John), and Christiansted (St. Croix). Private charters are the most flexible option for reaching remote sites like Carvel Rock and Congo Cay. The petroglyphs and plantation ruins on St. John are accessed via the national park with no boat required. BVI sites require a passport and a day trip booking; see the BVI day trip page for operators and logistics.

Planning note: Underwater rock formation sites (Carvel Rock, Congo Cay, Salt River Canyon) are weather-dependent — rough seas make boat trips to exposed sites uncomfortable and sometimes unsafe. Check forecasts; January through April typically brings the most settled conditions for offshore excursions.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are there sea caves in the US Virgin Islands?

The USVI does not have the type of accessible above-water sea caves that some Caribbean islands offer. The most celebrated sea caves in the region — The Caves at Norman Island and The Baths at Virgin Gorda — are in the British Virgin Islands and require a passport. The USVI has excellent underwater formations: canyon walls, rocky overhangs, and encrusted pier structures that function as cave-like dive environments. The Frederiksted Pier and Salt River Canyon are the standout examples.

What are The Baths at Virgin Gorda?

The Baths are massive granite boulders at the southern tip of Virgin Gorda (BVI) that have tumbled together to create grottos, pools, and tunnels at the shoreline. You explore them by wading and scrambling through the passages. They are the most visited site in the BVI and are accessible on a day trip from St. Thomas or St. John. A valid US passport is required.

What is Carvel Rock?

Carvel Rock is a sea stack south of St. Thomas — an isolated rock formation that rises from the water and creates a dive/snorkel site with ledges, overhangs, and strong marine life activity. Access is by boat only.

What are the Reef Bay petroglyphs?

Pre-Columbian rock carvings on St. John, located beside a freshwater pool at the base of the Reef Bay Trail. Attributed to the Taino people, they are among the only surviving pre-Columbian rock art in the USVI. Reached by hiking 2.2 miles or via NPS ranger-led guided hike.

Can you snorkel The Caves at Norman Island?

Yes — the cave openings are accessible from the water and snorkeling the cave mouths is the primary activity. Norman Island is in the BVI; passport required. Accessible on day trip boats from St. Thomas and St. John.

What is the Salt River Canyon on St. Croix?

A submarine canyon beginning close to St. Croix's shoreline that drops thousands of feet. Two wall dive profiles (East Wall and West Wall) make it one of the most dramatic dive sites in the territory. Also the site of Columbus's documented 1493 landing on US soil, within Salt River Bay NHP.

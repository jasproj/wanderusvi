Best Hikes & Trails in the US Virgin Islands

Updated October 2026 · WanderUSVI Tours you can book here →

The US Virgin Islands offer more quality hiking than almost any Caribbean island of equivalent size, concentrated primarily on St. John where roughly 60% of the land is Virgin Islands National Park. Over 20 maintained trails cross the park through tropical forest, past sugar plantation ruins, and down to national park beaches. The Reef Bay Trail to pre-Columbian rock carvings is the best individual hike in the territory; Ram Head gives the most dramatic cliff views; Bordeaux Mountain climbs to the island's high point through dense, verdant forest. St. Croix contributes a remote turtle beach walk and the short hike to the easternmost point in the USA. This guide covers the best trails on every island with distances, difficulty, and practical notes.

St. John — The Best Hiking Island in the Caribbean

No other Caribbean island offers the density of quality maintained hiking trails that St. John does, and the reason is straightforward: the national park. Virgin Islands National Park covers roughly 60% of St. John and maintains a trail network that ranges from easy 20-minute loops to strenuous half-day expeditions. Trails cross three distinct vegetation zones — dry coastal scrub, moist tropical forest, and the dense rainforest near Bordeaux Mountain. Many trails end at or connect to beaches, making a combination hike-and-swim day straightforward. For a comprehensive trail-by-trail breakdown, see our complete St. John hiking guide.

Reef Bay Trail

The signature hike in the USVI. The Reef Bay Trail descends 2.2 miles from Centerline Road through three vegetation zones — dry coastal forest, bay rum and kapok forest, and a more humid lower section — past the ruins of four sugar estates and a spur trail to the Reef Bay petroglyphs, ending at Reef Bay beach. The petroglyphs are pre-Columbian Taino carvings beside a freshwater pool — geometric forms and faces that are among the only surviving examples of pre-Columbian rock art in the USVI. Elevation loss is roughly 900 feet over the 2.2 miles — an easy-to-moderate descent, but a hard climb back up in the heat.

The NPS offers periodic ranger-led hikes that include interpretation at the plantation ruins and petroglyphs, and a boat return from Reef Bay beach directly to the Cruz Bay visitor center dock. This is strongly recommended: the boat return saves the uphill hike, and ranger interpretation transforms what might otherwise be unmarked ruins into a coherent historical narrative. Check the VINP website or visitor center for current program dates and reservations.

Ram Head Trail

The best trail in the USVI for dramatic views. Ram Head departs from Salt Pond Bay beach on St. John's south shore and climbs 1.8 miles round trip across cactus scrub and dry tropical forest to a volcanic basalt headland high above the sea. The geological character of the point — black rock, windswept vegetation, cliffs on three sides — is completely different from the lush green north shore. Views extend to St. Croix on the southern horizon, east toward Tortola and the BVI, and back across the bay to Salt Pond. Completely unshaded for the final third; start early and bring more water than you think you need. Excellent for sunset hikers if you can time the return to arrive back at the beach before dark.

Bordeaux Mountain Trail

The most physically demanding trail in the park leads to the highest point on St. John — around 1,277 feet above sea level. The trail descends steeply from Bordeaux Mountain Road through the densest, most humid forest on the island — enormous kapok trees, wild ferns, and a nearly continuous canopy that blocks the sun and traps humidity. The contrast with St. John's drier north shore is striking. The trail connects down to Lameshur Bay, a remote south-shore beach with no facilities and almost no visitors. A 4WD vehicle is recommended for the rough road to the trailhead. This is the most "jungle" trail experience available in the USVI — dark, steep, and magnificent.

Brown Bay Trail

The east end of St. John is the least-visited part of the island, and the Brown Bay Trail rewards the effort with a remote beach and some of the best shore snorkeling in the park — precisely because almost no one goes there. The trail is 1.6 miles one-way through dry forest; the return is the same path. Brown Bay beach itself is rocky (not a sunbathing destination) but the snorkeling off the rocks is exceptional, with healthy reef in clear, uncrowded water. No facilities; bring everything. This is an out-and-back with no bailout options, so start early and tell someone your plan.

Lind Point Trail

The most accessible trail in the park starts behind the VINP visitor center in Cruz Bay — no car needed. Lind Point is 1.1 miles one-way along a shaded coastal forest path to an overlook above Caneel Bay, or continue to Honeymoon Beach and Solomon Beach. The trail is essentially flat, well-maintained, and shaded. The Caneel Bay overlook frames one of the better views near Cruz Bay, with St. Thomas visible across the channel. Good for an early morning or late afternoon walk before or after the ferry. The path to Honeymoon Beach continues from the overlook for another 15-20 minutes and ends at a beach bar.

Cinnamon Bay Loop & Annaberg Plantation Loop

These two short loops add historical context to beach and snorkel days. The Cinnamon Bay loop (0.5 miles) passes through Danish plantation ruins — factory walls, a rum still base, and an old cemetery — before reaching the beach; most visitors walk right past the trailhead without noticing it. The Annaberg Plantation loop (0.25 miles) is the best-preserved sugar estate in the USVI: windmill tower, horse mill, boiling house, and slave quarters, with interpretive signage throughout. Both are appropriate for all fitness levels and can be combined with swimming or snorkeling at the adjacent beaches.

St. Croix Trails

St. Croix does not have the trail network of St. John, but two destinations are worth the walk. For everything St. Croix offers beyond the trails, see our guides to USVI parks and beaches.

Point Udall Walk

Point Udall is the easternmost point in the United States — a windswept limestone headland on St. Croix's eastern tip marked by a millennium monument. The walk from the small parking area to the point itself is short and easy. The draw is the view: unobstructed 180-degree exposure to the open Atlantic, with nothing between you and Europe. The geological character — limestone cliffs, crashing surf, no trees — is unlike any other viewpoint in the USVI. Isaac Bay, a remote turtle nesting beach, is accessible below the cliffs via a longer scramble. The approach road to the trailhead can be rough; a higher-clearance vehicle is useful though not strictly required in dry conditions.

Jack Bay Trail

Jack Bay Trail leads across St. Croix's east end to a remote beach that sees very few visitors. The trail is approximately 1.5 miles one-way through dry scrub forest — hot, exposed, and rocky underfoot. The reward is a wild beach at the end with excellent snorkeling off the rocky points and significant sea turtle activity (green and hawksbill turtles nest in this area). Continue beyond Jack Bay to Isaac Bay for even greater solitude. There are no facilities of any kind; bring all water, food, and sun protection. Avoid during active turtle nesting periods — check the USFWS website if visiting in spring or early summer.

Heat, Water & Safety

Hiking in the tropics requires a different approach than temperate-climate hiking. The most common hazard is dehydration combined with heat exhaustion — symptoms that escalate quickly in humid conditions without adequate water intake. The standard recommendation is at least 1 liter of water per person per hour of hiking, more in direct sun. There are no reliable water sources on any of these trails; carry everything you need from the trailhead.

Start every hike before 9 AM. The temperature and UV index from 10 AM to 3 PM can be genuinely dangerous on exposed sections like Ram Head or Jack Bay. Trails in forest shade (Reef Bay, Bordeaux Mountain) are more forgiving of midday starts but are still warm. Most trails in VINP have no cell service — download the NPS app map before leaving the visitor center. Closed-toe shoes or trail runners are appropriate for rocky trails; sandals work on the easy loops but not for Reef Bay, Ram Head, or Brown Bay.

Sea Turtle Etiquette on Nesting Beaches

Several trails in the USVI end at or pass through active sea turtle nesting beaches. Sandy Point NWR and the east end beaches of St. Croix (Jack Bay, Isaac Bay) are among the most important leatherback, green, and hawksbill nesting sites in the western hemisphere. When on these beaches: stay below the high-tide line, do not use any artificial light at night (including phone screens), do not approach or touch nesting turtles or hatchlings, and leave the beach before dark during active nesting periods. Sandy Point NWR is typically closed entirely during peak nesting; check the USFWS website before planning a visit.

When to Hike

December through April is the best season for hiking — dry weather, lower humidity, and consistent trade winds that cool the forest trails. February through April offers the clearest days and lowest rain probability. Summer (June through August) is hotter and more humid, with afternoon rain showers possible; trails are less crowded but the heat is significant. Hurricane season (June through November) can bring trail closures after storm events — check the VINP website for current conditions if visiting in this window.

Local tip: The VINP Visitor Center in Cruz Bay (right off the ferry dock) has free trail maps, current conditions, and schedules for ranger-led programs including the Reef Bay petroglyphs hike. Stop there before heading to any trailhead. Rangers can tell you exactly what is currently closed, what wildlife is active, and which trails have had recent maintenance.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best hike in the US Virgin Islands?

The Reef Bay Trail on St. John — 2.2 miles one-way through tropical forest past sugar plantation ruins and pre-Columbian petroglyphs, ending at a beach. The NPS ranger-led version includes a boat return and is highly recommended. Ram Head is the best for views.

Is St. John good for hiking?

Exceptional. About 60% of St. John is national park with 20+ maintained trails. No other Caribbean island of similar size offers this density of quality trails. Trails range from easy 20-minute loops to strenuous half-day hikes, and many end at beaches.

How hard is the Reef Bay Trail?

Moderate going down (2.2 miles, ~900 feet elevation loss); considerably harder returning uphill in heat. The NPS ranger-led hike includes a boat return from the beach, eliminating the uphill return — strongly recommended.

Are there hiking trails on St. Croix?

Limited but worthwhile. Point Udall is a short easy walk to the easternmost point in the USA. Jack Bay Trail leads to a remote turtle beach (~1.5 miles one-way, moderate). St. Croix is primarily a diving, beach, and history destination rather than a hiking island.

What should I bring on a USVI hike?

At minimum: 1+ liter of water per person per hour, reef-safe sunscreen, hat, and closed-toe shoes for rocky trails. Sandals work for easy loops. Download trail maps before leaving the visitor center; cell service is unavailable on most trails.

When is the best time to hike in the USVI?

December through April: dry, lower humidity, trade winds cooling the trails. Start before 9 AM year-round to avoid peak heat. Summer is hotter and more humid but trails are less crowded.

Can you hike to the Reef Bay petroglyphs independently?

Yes. The trail is open to self-guided hikers. Free maps at the VINP visitor center. The NPS also offers periodic ranger-led hikes with boat return — check the VINP website or visitor center for dates.

What sea turtle etiquette applies near St. Croix trails?

Stay below the high-tide line on nesting beaches, no artificial light at night, do not approach or touch turtles or hatchlings, leave before dark during active nesting. Sandy Point NWR closes during peak leatherback nesting — check USFWS website for current dates.
